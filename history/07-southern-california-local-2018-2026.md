# Southern California starting regions — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [California](04-california-2018-2026.md). **Focus:** Los Angeles Basin, Inland Empire, Cajon Pass, San Bernardino Mountains, Big Bear Valley, Victor Valley, Mojave River and nearby High Desert communities. **Separate speculative study:** [Southern California after the Great Plague](../regions/southern-california.md).

The purpose of this local prehistory is to identify people, infrastructure, employment and water systems in place **before** the fictional December 2026 AGI announcement. The economic and geographical baseline is not a claim that fictional population losses have already happened.

## 2018–2019: Metro basin and mountain/desert contrasts

Los Angeles/Long Beach ports, airports, professional services, film, finance, health, universities and international tourism support a vast metropolitan economy. The western Inland Empire is tied to that economy by commuting and logistics, but the rural mountains and high desert have different patterns of residence and employment. Interstate 15 connects the San Bernardino side of the basin to Cajon Pass, Victorville and onward toward Las Vegas; other mountain approaches exist and matter.

Big Bear Valley relies heavily on mountain tourism, recreation, service work, construction and seasonal property ownership. Its altitude, winter snow, short growing season, forest and steep routes mean that **access to equipment, fuel and imported groceries** is significant. The presence of Big Bear Lake does not make its domestic drinking supply a lake-water system.

Victor Valley includes Victorville, Hesperia, Apple Valley, Adelanto and smaller communities along the Mojave River and desert transport corridors. Distribution, commuting, retail, education, health services, defense-related facilities, construction and warehousing create varied local livelihoods. Daily movement between the high desert and metropolitan employers can be economically important even though the communities lie on the other side of the mountain barrier.

## 2020: The pandemic in a connected landscape

COVID-19 affects hospitals, schools, tourism, retail and commuting. The metro region connects quickly through airport travel, dense workplaces and households; mountain resort towns also have visitors, workers and seasonal residents. Desert suburbs connect through commuters, trucking, shops and shared services. It would be incorrect to assume **zero exposure** in Big Bear or Victor Valley simply because they have mountain or desert geography.

The pandemic produces layoffs and reductions in visitor activity while essential work continues. Restrictions and school operations differ by date and agency. For a later fictional epidemic, precise estimates should be built from age structure, households, social contacts, healthcare capacity, mobility and local policies rather than invented comparative percentages.

## 2021–2022: Housing, logistics, drought and water

Regional housing, remote work, logistics, construction and trucking encounter changing prices and labor demands. The Inland Empire is a major freight/distribution corridor and is strongly affected by changing international imports and consumer demand. Mountain communities continue experiencing tourism demand and seasonal travel pressures.

**Big Bear water:** the City of Big Bear Lake Department of Water & Power states that its drinking water comes from **local groundwater, wells and springs**, not directly from the lake, and is not imported from off mountain. The recharge supply depends on precipitation and infiltration in an isolated mountain watershed. This creates a very different post-Cascade vulnerability from a municipal district relying on long-distance aqueducts.

**Mojave Basin:** a water-rights adjudication culminated in a 1996 judgment, with Mojave Water Agency operating as Watermaster. Its oversight includes reported groundwater extraction, assessments and rights transfers. The legal and physical consequences of pumps losing electric power would vary by wells, local controllers, generators, groundwater depth and network dependence. **An adjudicated right is not a physically operating pump.**

## 2023–2024: Storms, routes and differential recovery

California's 2022–2023 atmospheric river sequence brings substantial precipitation and snow, but not identical recharge in the San Bernardino Mountains, Victor Valley and the wider Mojave. Winter weather can block routes, while floods, debris, rockfall or road deterioration create different seasonal impediments.

A high-desert settlement with extensive roads and distribution yards may retain salvage, materials and workshop capacity, yet its water and food supply can be less self-sufficient than one might infer from its size. Big Bear may have local springs and wells but face steep terrain, winter, limited farmland and dependence on imported food. The metro region has extraordinary hospitals, workforce and industrial infrastructure but huge concentrations of people and highly interdependent utilities.

## 2025–November 2026: Fire, economic exposure and technological transition

The January 2025 Eaton and Palisades fires cause severe damage in Los Angeles County, but **not in every locality**: those specific fire footprints should not be transferred to Victorville, Hesperia or Big Bear. They do, however, show the consequences of fire, strong winds, evacuation difficulties, utility hazards and regional demand for emergency crews, housing and insurance.

AI-assisted office work, logistics systems, customer service, data-center infrastructure and warehouse automation increasingly form part of the economy. Local workers may be employed by distant metropolitan institutions. The fictional 2027–2029 labor crisis must distinguish remote-work exposure, warehouse logistics, physical trades, hospitality, care and vehicle maintenance.

By November 17, 2026, neither the fictional AGI announcement nor the Great Plague has occurred. Regions do not yet have the post-collapse borders or successor governments described as provisional future possibilities in the regional scenario.

## Local focus A: Big Bear Valley

**Settlements:** Big Bear Lake, Big Bear City, Fawnskin, Baldwin Lake and related mountain localities. **Geographical constraints:** altitude, lake and separate groundwater system, timber/forest, winter snow, fire, access roads and limited agricultural growing season. **Employment:** tourism, retail, lodging, services, education, maintenance, construction and residents commuting or working remotely.

**Infrastructure questions:** Map the exact sources, well locations, tank storage, booster stations, treatment, pipe networks, springs and water districts separately. Identify which services use electric pumps and which use gravity. Establish grocery and fuel replenishment routes in winter, healthcare facilities and emergency transport across the mountains. Identify cold-storage capacity, local food and livestock feed rather than asserting the valley could feed an arbitrarily large refugee population.

**Historical exposure question:** distinguish visitors, second-home owners and permanent residents, particularly during holidays. Tourist visitation can bring infection even when permanent population is small.

## Local focus B: Cajon Pass and movement corridors

The pass is a major I-15 and railway corridor between the Inland Empire and Victor Valley, but **is not the only route** into all mountain and high-desert communities. US 18/CA 18, CA 138, CA 247 and other highways have different approaches, grades, connections and maintenance requirements. No specific fictional quarantine, checkpoint date or enforcing force is established in the 2018–2026 baseline.

Before inventing the Cajon Quarantine, map road and railway approaches, plausible diversions, available personnel and legal authority; test whether restrictions would meaningfully reduce introductions or simply redirect travel elsewhere.

## Local focus C: Victor Valley and Mojave River

**Settlements:** Victorville, Hesperia, Apple Valley, Adelanto, Oro Grande, Helendale and nearby communities. The **Mojave River is not a reliable continuously flowing surface-water source**; groundwater basins, infiltration, pumping and imported or stored supplies must be distinguished. Watermaster reports cover extraction rights and production from many wells.

**Supply and labor:** identify commuting, trucking, industrial yards, retail, health institutions, food warehouses, rail, road and aviation facilities, schools and municipal services. Warehouses are stockpiles only while they contain supplies and can be accessed; an urban landscape does not automatically imply permanently useful salvaged machinery. Determine which neighborhoods and workplaces depend on pumped water, cooling and imported food.

**Future scenario link:** the previously approved broad direction includes surviving Big Bear and Victor Valley communities and substantial LA Basin losses after the fictional Plague. It is not a factual description of any real event through November 2026 and should not overwrite this prehistory.

## Outstanding research register

Prioritize a location-specific map of aquifers and water districts; 2018/2020/2025 wildfire footprints and evacuation zones; hospitals and clinics with travel times; 2018–2026 demographic trend by town; major employer sectors; transport chokepoints and alternate routes; electric utility networks; seasonal groceries and fuel; physical agricultural potential. For each element preserve source date, jurisdiction and uncertainty.
