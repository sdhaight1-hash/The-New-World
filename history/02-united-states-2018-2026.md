# United States — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [Western Hemisphere](01-western-hemisphere-2018-2026.md). **Next:** [Western United States](03-western-united-states-2018-2026.md). Focus here is on federal institutions, national economic and demographic systems, energy, infrastructure, political events, security and disasters that affect all states differently.

## 2018–2019: Trade, jobs, disasters and federalism

US tariffs on Chinese goods and China's retaliation reshape manufacturing and farming trade relationships, while financial markets and employers adjust. The economy remains strongly service-oriented and geographically uneven, with coastal technology and finance, inland agriculture, the industrial Midwest, energy regions and tourism dependent on different labor and resource systems.

The **2018 Camp Fire** in California destroys much of Paradise and becomes a defining disaster for wildfire evacuation, electric utilities and insurance. Hurricanes, floods and severe storms illustrate other regional hazards. The US political system has federal, state, county, municipal and tribal authorities with separate rights and responsibilities. None can automatically be substituted for another in later local history.

## 2020: COVID-19, emergency measures, protests and election

COVID-19 reaches the US; federal, state and local emergency responses differ. Hospitals and long-term care confront major pressures. School systems change operations and remote instruction expands unevenly. Congress enacts large-scale relief measures including the CARES Act; the Federal Reserve intervenes in credit markets. Unemployment rises sharply during early pandemic shutdowns before an uneven recovery.

The killing of George Floyd in May prompts nationwide protests against police violence and racism, with concurrent debates about policing, public order and reform. Demonstrations and counter-demonstrations have different local circumstances. The November election is won by Joe Biden. Proposals about the legitimacy of future institutions must be grounded in documented actions, law and local responses, not projected backward from a fictional collapse.

Western wildfires and Atlantic hurricanes make 2020 unusually destructive. NOAA's contemporaneous assessment counted **22 separate US billion-dollar weather and climate disasters**, with counts and adjusted losses tied to that reporting vintage.

## 2021: Transition, recovery and infrastructure

Joe Biden is inaugurated in January. A violent crowd attacks the US Capitol on **6 January 2021** during congressional certification of the 2020 election; subsequent criminal, political and legal consequences unfold over following years. COVID-19 vaccination, recovery, school reopening and employment shifts continue. Congress passes the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act in November, launching longer-term federal spending on transport, broadband, energy and other assets.

The military withdrawal from Afghanistan is completed in August. Severe heat and fires affect the West; Hurricane Ida affects the Gulf and Northeast. Supply-chain stress, shipping delays and semiconductor shortages constrain production. Federal infrastructure spending creates a baseline of planned or partially completed projects; it does not ensure every bridge, broadband project or water system is finished by 2026.

## 2022: Inflation, rates and industrial policy

The US economy faces inflation driven by multiple interacting factors, including pandemic disruptions, recovering demand and war-related commodity shocks. The Federal Reserve raises the policy rate during 2022 and 2023. The CHIPS and Science Act and Inflation Reduction Act create incentives and policy changes affecting semiconductors, energy, manufacturing and infrastructure. The Supreme Court's Dobbs decision overturns Roe v. Wade, returning substantial abortion policy authority to states, which then diverge in law and practice.

Russian warfare in Ukraine contributes to security assistance, sanctions and defense industrial demand. ChatGPT's launch in November starts a new widely accessible consumer and workplace AI era.

## 2023–2024: Domestic variation and changing technology

Employment and inflation evolve unevenly. Housing affordability and interest rates affect household formation and migration. Cross-border policy, opioid overdoses, national political debates, education, insurance and energy remain issues with major geographic differences. The 2023 Lahaina wildfire in Hawaii, severe storms and flooding elsewhere, and subsequent major US weather disasters underline that infrastructure resilience is an ongoing concern.

The 2024 election returns Donald Trump to the presidency. Investment in chips, computing, data centers and artificial intelligence grows in several regions. The key distinction for the setting is between increased automation of **tasks** and the fictional **mass workforce displacement** that begins after the December 2026 AGI divergence.

## 2025–17 November 2026: Federal policy and division of authority

Donald Trump assumes office in January 2025; tariffs, industrial policy, immigration enforcement, foreign affairs and energy policy are contested across institutions and businesses. Outcomes vary by legal process, state, sector and time. The US and Israel strike Iran in February 2026, and the conflict complicates energy and security calculations.

On **3 November 2026**, midterm elections lead the Associated Press to call Democratic control of the House and Senate. Some individual counts may remain pending certification as of November 17; the incoming Congress does not take office until January 2027. Speculative legislative responses after an AGI announcement in December must not be reported as historical fact.

## National geography of critical systems

The US food system connects irrigation regions, grain belts, feedlots, processing plants, trucking and refrigerated chains. Electricity is generated and moved through regional grids; petroleum and natural gas move through pipelines, terminals, rail and truck. Hospitals require power, medicines, sterilization, trained staff and communications. Internet and financial services rely on data centers, communications and secure infrastructure. Military readiness requires industrial supply, personnel and functioning command systems.

State power, local government, tribal sovereignty, private ownership and federal regulation are separate dimensions. In a fictional crisis, losing one capacity does not automatically transfer legal title or legitimacy to whichever armed group controls a road.

## Disaster memory and geography

NOAA identified **28 billion-dollar US disaster events in 2023** and **27 in 2024**, using its contemporaneous definitions and inflation-adjustment vintage. The precise number is not a natural constant; data and methods change. Events include hurricanes, floods, severe storms, drought, fire and cold-weather emergencies. Different regions build different preparations and residual vulnerabilities.
