# Oregon — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [Western United States](03-western-united-states-2018-2026.md). **Next:** [Clatsop County and the northern Oregon coast](08-clatsop-local-2018-2026.md).

## 2018–2019: A divided geographical economy

Oregon's Pacific coast, Coast Range, Willamette Valley, Cascade Mountains and eastern interior are distinct economic and ecological regions. The Portland metro area contains manufacturing, technology, health, education, freight and professional services; the Willamette Valley grows crops and hosts industry; coastal counties rely in varying proportions on tourism, fisheries, timber, education and public employment. Eastern Oregon includes ranching, farming and energy systems with different water and winter hazards.

The Columbia River links ocean shipping to ports, inland farms, timber, hydroelectric power and industries. Interstate 5 and US 101 serve different purposes. The Astoria–Warrenton–Seaside corridor cannot simply be described as another Portland suburb.

In 2018 Oregon emergency management records a North Santiam River cyanotoxin incident affecting water management and public communication. Winter storms, wildfire, flood and coastal seismic/tsunami preparedness predate the fictional collapse.

## 2020: COVID-19 and Labor Day fires

COVID-19 changes tourism, schools, hospitality, health systems and services. Effects are geographically uneven. The Coast depends on seasonal visitors from inland metros, which may expose communities to illness while supplying income.

The **September 7–8, 2020 Labor Day windstorm** spreads multiple megafires across Oregon. The Oregon Department of Energy reports more than a million acres burned in a matter of days. Wind, vegetation, infrastructure and evacuation geography matter. Loss of local fuel access affects firefighting and utility repair even where the regional fuel system remains physically available.

This is a useful demonstration of a critical systems principle: an intact regional supply chain can still be unavailable to people at a damaged local point of use.

## 2021: The Pacific Northwest heat dome

Late June 2021 delivers unprecedented modern temperature records. Portland airport reaches **116°F on June 28**; Oregon's heat and drought burden is severe. Heat exposure varies with housing, age, air conditioning, health, outdoor work and location. Coastal conditions differ from interior valleys and high deserts, so do not assign Portland Airport's temperature to Astoria or Seaside.

The same year demonstrates the importance of cooling, water, electricity and medical logistics; later fictional losses of dependable electricity would matter differently in normally temperate and already heat-adapted communities.

## 2022–2023: Recovery, housing and infrastructure

Oregon continues recovering from the pandemic and 2020 fires while facing inflation, housing availability, labor shortages in some sectors and changing remote-work patterns. Pacific trade, road maintenance and storm impacts connect urban and rural economies. Coastal hazard planning continues around the Cascadia Subduction Zone and local tsunami evacuation infrastructure; **the real Cascadia earthquake hazard is not The New World's fictional solar Cascade**.

The north coast's relative remoteness is qualified by US 101, US 26, the Astoria–Megler Bridge, tourism and shipping. Highway condition, access to fuel and bridges are more important to later resilience than a vague label of rural isolation.

## 2024: Large wildfire season and governance

Oregon's Department of Forestry reports approximately **1.9 million acres burned in 2024**, exceeding earlier state records under its reporting approach. Effects concentrate geographically, while Oregon continues preparing for coastal hazards and infrastructure risks. Economic and political debates over timber, housing, wildfire management and emissions differ between the Portland region, coast and east.

## 2025–November 2026: Technology investment and local economies

Oregon's semiconductor ecosystem is centered largely in the Portland metro region, not in every town. A July 2026 Business Oregon assessment identifies large employment and export contributions while warning about industrial competition and investment challenges. These assets shape statewide tax bases, electricity demand, transport and skilled-worker geography; they should not be projected onto coastal settlements without explicit economic links.

The coastal counties retain fishing, shellfish, logging, marine service, schools and tourist infrastructure. Income from one sector is not interchangeable with a reliable emergency food supply. Oregon's state and local disaster agencies maintain plans for wildfire, winter storms, heat, landslides, floods and coastal earthquake/tsunami risk.

## Material and institutional baseline for The New World

**Western valleys and coast:** rainfall and river networks, forestry, ports and tourism. **Cascades:** snow and pass routes, hydropower and forest ecosystems. **Eastern Oregon:** grazing, irrigation, lower population density and longer medical/transport distances. **Urban semiconductor corridor:** specialized knowledge and imported machinery. **Jurisdictions:** tribal nations, the state, counties, port districts, cities, water districts and federal land agencies.

The fictional 2030 Plague is a **different disease**, and the solar Cascade is a **different hazard** from a Cascadia earthquake. Both future events should be modeled using the actual people, roads, utilities and institutions inherited from this historical baseline.
