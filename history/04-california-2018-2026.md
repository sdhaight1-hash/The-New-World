# California — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [Western United States](03-western-united-states-2018-2026.md). **Next:** [Southern California and starting regions](07-southern-california-local-2018-2026.md). This is a state-wide historical layer, deliberately more detailed than the national account but less detailed than Big Bear, Victor Valley and Cajon Pass.

## Economic and geographical foundation in 2018

California's economy combines international trade, technology and media production, manufacturing, health services, tourism, agriculture, logistics, energy and defense. Los Angeles/Long Beach ports are key entry points for Pacific trade; inland warehouses, rail and trucking link imported goods to the rest of the US. The Bay Area leads in computing and venture capital, while the Central Valley relies heavily on irrigation, farms, processing and migrant labor. Coastal, Sierra Nevada and desert systems have substantially different rain, snow and groundwater.

Water does not come from one source. Urban and agricultural supplies involve local wells, Sierra Nevada runoff, reservoirs, canals, Colorado River allocations, groundwater regulation and conservation. Local rights and pumping equipment matter just as much as aggregate state storage.

In 2018 wildfires devastate multiple areas. The Camp Fire destroys much of Paradise and kills 85 people; the Woolsey Fire burns in Southern California. Evacuation, home insurance, utility operations and wildland–urban interfaces become central political and engineering concerns.

## 2019–2020: Housing, jobs, pandemic and unprecedented fires

California's housing affordability, homelessness, migration, tech employment, agricultural labor and urban/suburban commuting form an unequal pre-pandemic baseline. COVID-19 closures and public-health measures disrupt entertainment, tourism, schools, universities and hospitality; essential workers continue physical jobs and face different exposures.

In 2020, extreme fire conditions produce California's largest burned area in the state's modern recorded history, more than **4.3 million acres**. Some fires destroy built communities, while others burn remote terrain; these impacts must not be treated as identical. Smoke, utility interruptions and evacuations overlap with the pandemic. Government action and local responses vary by county.

## 2021–2022: Drought, economy, rates and water

Drought, heat and wildfire risks constrain agriculture and regional water planning. The state's diverse labor market experiences pandemic recovery and inflation differently in restaurants, film, technology, warehousing, hospitals and agriculture. Higher interest rates affect real estate, business investment and household budgets. The state maintains distinct policies on climate, housing, employment and utilities in relation to the federal government.

California's 2014 Sustainable Groundwater Management Act drives groundwater planning in numerous basins; **adjudicated basins** such as the Mojave Basin Area have their own legal frameworks. Colorado River agreements and imported-water arrangements differ from local mountain springs or deep desert wells.

## 2023: Atmospheric rivers, flood and snow

Repeated atmospheric river storms beginning late December 2022 and continuing into 2023 bring major precipitation, heavy Sierra snowpack and flooding. The water year replenishes some supplies but damages roads, homes and farms. The significance of the wet year differs sharply between Central Valley farms, high mountains, coasts and arid desert basins; one wet year does not erase structural groundwater limits.

Highways through the Coast Ranges and mountains can close from landslides, storms or snow. These are critical future transport questions for a later era when road crews, fuel and bridge repair may be scarce.

## 2024: Technology, housing and grid pressures

Semiconductors, enterprise software, AI research and creative technology remain concentrated in portions of the state. Data-center and electrical demand rise alongside other energy-policy goals. National-level arguments about immigration, environmental regulation, policing, health and education produce very different local results. Do not treat Silicon Valley's employment pattern as representative of the Central Valley, Inland Empire or rural counties.

## 2025: Major Los Angeles fires

On **January 7, 2025**, the Palisades and Eaton fires begin in Los Angeles County. Los Angeles County's later fact sheet records **23,448 acres and 6,837 destroyed structures** for the Palisades Fire, and **14,021 acres and 9,414 destroyed structures** for the Eaton Fire. It lists 12 and 19 confirmed fatalities respectively. The fires have broad recovery, housing, workforce, insurance and public-health consequences. Destruction is concentrated in particular communities; it must not be applied to every Los Angeles neighborhood or to our high-desert study.

These disasters illustrate the vulnerability of electrical distribution, evacuation roads, vegetation interfaces and built housing stock, but do not imply that the later fictional Cascade is an ordinary wildfire.

## 2026 through November 17

California enters the fictional AGI divergence with highly advanced computing and global trade, a large and diverse labor force, powerful public and private institutions, expensive housing, agricultural dependency on water, and regions where tourism, logistics and local services dominate employment. The state remains geographically, economically and politically heterogeneous.

A hypothetical 2027–2029 surge in automation would probably interact differently with professional software work, retail service, animation, port logistics, agriculture, health care and manual construction. **Those are scenario questions**, not real-world job-loss statistics for 2026. Any claims about individual counties or employers require sourced research.

## Pre-collapse inventory to carry into the local study

- Road hierarchy: I-5, I-10, I-15, I-40, major passes, freight corridors and parallel routes.
- Water hierarchy: Sierra reservoirs/canals, municipal systems, regional adjudication, isolated aquifers, wells, pumps and maintenance.
- Food and labor: farms, processing plants, seasonal workers, imports, stored food and veterinary services.
- Public-health geography: regional hospitals, clinics, care facilities and travel times from remote communities.
- Institutions: state, county, tribal and municipal authorities, utility companies, special districts, port authorities and emergency responders.
- Hazard memory: fire, drought, extreme precipitation, heat, landslide, snow, earthquake and coastal exposure.
- Technology assets: data centers, repair workshops, telecom exchanges, automation warehouses, machine shops, training colleges and industrial sites.
