# Western United States — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [United States](02-united-states-2018-2026.md). **Next:** [California](04-california-2018-2026.md) · [Oregon](05-oregon-2018-2026.md) · [Wyoming](06-wyoming-2018-2026.md). The 'West' is a geographic analysis region rather than an official government. Coastal urban systems, interior valleys, mountains, reservations, Great Basin deserts and energy-producing plains have distinct constraints.

## 2018–2019: Fire, power and uneven growth

California's 2018 fires, including the Camp and Woolsey fires, show how wind, fuels, settlement at the wildland–urban interface and utility systems can combine disastrously. Camp Fire devastates Paradise; the state's public-utility and insurance institutions confront long-term consequences. Oregon and inland Western states experience their own wildfire, drought and rural-development pressures, which are not interchangeable.

Western cities expand technology, warehousing, tourism, service and health-care employment while rural communities remain dependent on farming, energy extraction, timber, tourism and government services in varied proportions. Housing costs and migration reshape metropolitan and small-town populations. Federal forests, public lands, national parks, tribal reservations and military installations affect economic organization and land jurisdiction.

## 2020: COVID-19 arrives in a region of complex mobility

Public-health responses change schools, universities, hospitals, resort towns and border crossings. Coastal metros with international airports are highly interconnected, but mountain and rural places also have tourists, commuters, service workers and essential transport. **Isolation is a variable, not a magic shield against epidemics.**

The western 2020 fire season is extraordinary. California burns more than four million acres; severe fires occur across Oregon and Washington. Smoke travels across state borders; evacuation, insurance, utilities and emergency services are stressed simultaneously with pandemic conditions. Hazard impacts depend on exact watersheds, roads, elevations, prevailing winds and local settlement geography.

## 2021: Heat dome, drought and water anxiety

The Pacific Northwest heat dome peaks in late June. Portland Airport reaches **116°F on June 28, 2021**; the heat is deadly and affects infrastructure, agriculture and populations with unequal access to cooling. In the Great Basin, Colorado River watershed and interior West, drought and water allocations remain politically and economically significant. Western agriculture often relies on irrigation and engineered deliveries rather than local rainfall alone.

Snowpack influences river flows, reservoirs, hydropower, hydroelectric storage and downstream deliveries. A single wet year cannot be extrapolated as a permanently solved water problem; a region's pre-Cascade water portfolio needs separate hydrological accounting.

## 2022–2023: A changing water and industrial landscape

Inflation, interest rates and housing affordability affect construction, tourism and logistics. Much of the West experiences drought and wildfire risk; the extraordinary 2022–2023 California storm sequence replenishes some snowpack and reservoirs while causing flooding, landslides, road damage and emergency evacuations. State and local water agencies continue managing long-term overdraft, compacts and environmental requirements.

Industrial and federal investment in semiconductor facilities, batteries, renewable energy, defense and cloud infrastructure raises the importance of power, specialized labor and transport. California's technology centers, Oregon's semiconductor ecosystem, Southwest logistics and Wyoming's energy extraction perform different roles in these networks.

## 2024–November 2026: Urban AI investment and uneven physical resilience

High-performance computing expands in selected metropolitan and industrial centers, while many rural areas depend on existing agricultural, mechanical, timber, extraction and tourism skills. Wildfire remains severe: Oregon's 2024 fire season burns roughly 1.9 million acres according to the state forestry agency. Los Angeles County's January 2025 Eaton and Palisades fires destroy thousands of structures and cause deaths and displacement. Costs, counts and exposure must be linked to reporting year and agency.

As of November 17, 2026, Western states retain different political and legal systems and different federal relationships. National elections do not produce uniform local consequences. The fictional AGI era after December 2026 could accelerate automation of Pacific-region office work, industrial logistics, energy monitoring and robotics, but adoption and job losses must be researched *by sector and place*, not filled with one regional percentage.

## Physical systems inherited by the speculative future

**Water:** snowpack, watersheds, interstate river agreements, reservoirs, groundwater adjudication, irrigation, treatment, pumps and local wells. Big Bear's drinking water depends on groundwater; Victor Valley's groundwater is governed by detailed rights and watermaster oversight.

**Transport:** Pacific ports, interstates 5/10/15/80/84/90 and local mountain roads; rail freight, airports and ferries where applicable. A pass closure affects trade according to whether alternate routes, fuel, bridges and crews survive.

**Energy:** coastal grids, hydropower, hydrocarbon pipelines, solar, wind, Wyoming coal/gas, electrical transmission and backup generation. Ownership, operation and grid connectivity are different claims.

**Land and institutions:** federal and state forests, ranch property, water districts, tribal sovereignty, municipalities, unincorporated communities and counties. Public authority does not disappear simply because one road or transformer fails.

**Demography:** workforce migration, retirement, tourism seasons, migrant agricultural labor, urban/suburban commuting and rural health-care access create differing pre-Plague vulnerabilities. Do not assume a city dies or a ranch survives solely because of settlement density.
