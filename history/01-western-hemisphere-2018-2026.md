# Western Hemisphere — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [Global history](00-world-2018-2026.md). **Next:** [United States](02-united-states-2018-2026.md). This account adds North, Central and South America and the Caribbean without treating them as a single economic or political community.

## 2018–2019: Different economies, interconnected migration

The United States, Canada and Mexico share closely connected manufacturing, agricultural, transport and energy systems, but different governing institutions, welfare systems, exchange rates and immigration regimes. Negotiation of the US–Mexico–Canada trade agreement in 2018, following NAFTA's long history, signals both continued integration and disputes over its terms. US–China tariffs affect North American exporters and manufacturers while agricultural markets adjust.

Mexico's Andrés Manuel López Obrador begins his presidency in December 2018. Brazil's Jair Bolsonaro assumes office in January 2019. Venezuela's political and economic crisis contributes to mass outward movement; migrants and refugees find destinations across Colombia, Peru, Brazil, Ecuador, Chile and beyond. The dynamics differ considerably from those governing movement from Central America and Mexico to the US.

2019 protests in Chile, Bolivia and elsewhere arise from distinct local political and economic disputes. Caribbean states face hurricane exposure, tourism dependence and infrastructure vulnerabilities without sharing a single fiscal or institutional trajectory.

## 2020–2021: Pandemic response and unequal economic shock

COVID-19 reaches every part of the hemisphere, but disease burden, testing, vaccination, public assistance and hospital capacity differ. Canada, the US, Mexico, Brazil and other states adopt divergent public-health measures and fiscal responses. Tourism-dependent Caribbean communities, informal workers in Latin America, border cities, migrant households, essential logistics workers and remote rural areas experience different constraints.

Supply shortages and international tourism decline reverberate through ports, airports, factories and service economies. Border restrictions affect cross-border households and business. The USMCA enters into force on **1 July 2020**, preserving a framework for deeply integrated North American trade while creating new rules for labor, automotive manufacturing and regional content. Pandemic-era recovery in 2021 brings uneven inflation and employment changes. The combination of job insecurity and rising prices is an important regional baseline, not a universal path to political failure.

Canadian, US and Mexican supply chains still run through many globally sourced chips, fuels, chemicals, vehicles and machines. A future loss of national-grid reliability or international components would have disparate effects on factories, farms and small communities.

## 2022–2023: Commodity prices, political changes and migration

Russia's war in Ukraine raises food, fertilizer, oil and gas uncertainties. Energy producers and agricultural exporters may benefit from higher prices while import-dependent households face higher costs. Latin American governments make distinct policy choices amid inflation, debt, security issues, inequality and public demands for services.

Brazil's 2022 election brings Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva back to the presidency in January 2023. The US–Mexico border remains central to migration and security debates. Venezuela's displacement continues to affect many host states, and the Darién Gap crossing becomes a major humanitarian concern. This cannot be reduced to one uniform 'migration wave': origin countries, reasons for movement, legal categories, routes and reception policies vary substantially.

Canada experiences its unprecedented 2023 wildfire season, with large-scale smoke spreading into the US. Smoke and disruption demonstrate how environmental exposure crosses political borders and affects health and transportation even far from the fires.

## 2024: Continental political divergence

Mexico elects Claudia Sheinbaum as president in June 2024; she is inaugurated in October, succeeding López Obrador. The United States elects Donald Trump in November 2024. Canada's political trajectory and the Caribbean's tourism and disaster planning remain separate historical questions rather than derivatives of US elections.

In 2024 the Panama Canal, Caribbean trade routes, ports, cross-border trucking, energy pipelines and agricultural exports remain essential continental connections. Their importance lies in the goods, workers, fuel and capital they move—not in hypothetical later political allegiance.

## 2025–November 2026: Trade, energy and institutional exposure

North American firms and governments contend with changing tariffs, industrial policy, border measures and competition for advanced semiconductor and AI investment. The North American economy is deeply interconnected but not self-sufficient: individual factories depend on cross-border inputs, electricity, skills and maintenance.

UNHCR's December 2025 account describes **nearly 7.9 million Venezuelan refugees and migrants worldwide**, with most in Latin America and the Caribbean. This is a dated measure, not a fictional 2030 population map. Major wildfires, storms and floods in North America create repeated tests of emergency management and insurance. The US political landscape changes again with the 2026 midterm elections, while Latin American and Canadian policies remain independently variable.

## The hemisphere's structural geography

**North America:** United States and Canada have extensive transport, electricity, telecoms, health systems and industrial supply networks; Mexico is a central manufacturing and migration partner. The Great Lakes, Mississippi basin, Pacific ports, Gulf of Mexico, Colorado River and major cross-border routes matter to material flows.

**Central America, Caribbean and Panama:** canal shipping, remittances, port services, agriculture, tourism, tropical storms, seismic risk and energy imports vary by locality. A coastal resort does not inherit the same resource constraints as an inland agricultural district.

**Northern South America:** Andean corridors, Caribbean ports, oil production, displaced populations and major cities link countries while creating distinct security and medical capacities.

**Amazon basin and Brazil:** watersheds, river transport, soils, forest ecology, hydroelectric systems and megacities deserve separate regional treatment. Large energy and agricultural capacity does not imply straightforward distribution after a hypothetical infrastructure failure.

**Southern cone:** Argentina, Chile, Uruguay and neighbors have separate institutional histories, agricultural systems, mining, renewable-energy investments, mountain passes and maritime routes.

## Questions that matter when the fictional world diverges

What share of food, fertilizer, fuel, electronics and medicine comes from outside each region? Which populations have recent migration networks? Which authorities have practical control over border crossings? Where are hospitals, grids, dams, refineries and ports? Which communities have reliable local water and professional skills? Do not transplant US-style local politics, tribal governance or land-tenure systems into other countries.
