# Global history — 1 January 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Scope:** worldwide; documented pre-divergence history only. **Next:** [Western Hemisphere](01-western-hemisphere-2018-2026.md) · [Earlier detailed world chronology, 2019–2026](../bible/21-real-world-2019-2026.md). **Later alternate-history divergence:** December 2026 AGI announcement.

This is the broadest historical layer. It records processes with global reach and representative defining events, not a catalogue of every election, strike, flood or skirmish. It is an account of the actual inherited world, not an assertion that the fictional Great Plague or Cascade was inevitable.

## 2018 — Global integration under growing stress

A decade after the financial crisis, global commerce still links economies through trade finance, international shipping, aviation, tourism, digital services and highly specialized manufacturing. The United States and China begin escalating tariffs against each other in 2018. The dispute draws attention to industrial policy, intellectual property, technology supply and strategic dependence. The withdrawal of the United States from the Iran nuclear agreement in May and renewed sanctions affect international diplomacy and energy markets. The US and North Korea hold a summit in June, illustrating coexistence of diplomacy and strategic mistrust. Britain continues negotiating its departure from the European Union.

In climate history, severe wildfires, heatwaves, flooding and storms show that regionally concentrated disasters can have consequences beyond their immediate boundaries. The IPCC's October 2018 report on 1.5°C warming highlights the increasing importance of heat, water, emissions policy and adaptation. Across societies, smartphones, cloud infrastructure, platforms, online payments and algorithms already organize much of daily life, although sophisticated general-purpose conversational AI is not yet publicly accessible.

**What persists into the speculative era:** interdependence, disputes over trade and technology, and the necessity of both physical and digital infrastructure.

## 2019 — Social unrest and the last pre-pandemic year

US–China trade disputes continue, Britain remains preoccupied with Brexit, and protests affect Hong Kong, Chile, Lebanon and other societies for different local reasons. None is proof of one unified worldwide movement. The world's political baseline is plural: elections, authoritarian institutions, protests, civil society, social protection and economic performance differ dramatically.

Seaborne and airborne transport, commodity markets and manufacturing remain highly international. In December the WHO receives reports of pneumonia cases in Wuhan, launching the later COVID-19 historical sequence. The economic and public-health consequences still lie ahead.

## 2020 — Pandemic, global recession and international travel disruption

COVID-19 spreads. WHO declares a public-health emergency on 30 January and characterizes a pandemic on 11 March. Governments adopt varying combinations of travel controls, emergency health measures, restrictions, income support and testing. Hospitals, care facilities, schools, employers and families are affected differently. The world experiences a deep recession, alongside extraordinary fiscal and central-bank interventions; aviation, tourism and in-person services face especially sharp disruption.

Global supply chains encounter shortages and bottlenecks. Remote work, education and ecommerce expand where broadband and occupations permit, while agriculture, logistics, health care and essential physical labor cannot simply move online. In the US, Joe Biden wins the presidential election. In Europe, the UK formally leaves the EU in January, beginning a new trade and institutional period.

**For later history:** document the survival of public-health staff, emergency management institutions, data networks, local trust and household savings. Different pandemic experiences can shape responses to an unrelated fictional disease around 2030.

## 2021 — Uneven reopening and inflationary pressures

Vaccines are introduced and administered at very different rates, while COVID-19 waves continue. Economies recover unevenly. Manufacturing bottlenecks, crowded ports, shortages and recovering demand contribute to inflationary pressures. Shortages of semiconductors affect cars, electronics and specialized machinery. Remote work and cloud services become enduring components of some labor markets.

The Taliban take Kabul in August following the collapse of the former Afghan government during US/allied withdrawal. Migration, human rights, state capacity and international commitments become important security questions. Severe heat, droughts, floods and fires affect multiple parts of the world, including the Pacific Northwest and Western Canada.

## 2022 — Major war, food and energy shocks, inflation and ChatGPT

Russia launches a full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February, intensifying the conflict that began in 2014. Fighting, mass displacement, sanctions, international assistance and disrupted energy and food flows profoundly influence the global economy and European security. Many central banks raise interest rates in response to inflation. Concerns about chip supply, critical minerals, energy reliability and industrial security increase.

On 30 November, OpenAI releases ChatGPT publicly. This is a pivotal technological event, though not the beginning of this historical record. Generative AI becomes widely approachable through natural-language interaction.

## 2023 — Widening conflict and AI capabilities

War in Ukraine continues. Finland joins NATO in April. Fighting erupts in Sudan in April and produces severe humanitarian consequences. Hamas-led attacks in Israel on 7 October and Israel's subsequent operations in Gaza cause extensive casualties, destruction and displacement; tensions affect the wider Middle East. Houthi attacks on commercial vessels lead carriers to divert shipping away from the Red Sea route.

The WHO ends COVID-19's international emergency designation in May, not the disease itself. GPT-4 is introduced in March; other generative tools expand. Adoption and training of workers remain uneven. Governments confront competing priorities involving AI governance, investment, privacy and labor.

## 2024 — Elections, heat, AI regulation and trade vulnerability

Sweden joins NATO in March. The EU's AI Act enters into force in August with phased requirements. GPT-4o and competitor multimodal systems demonstrate changing interaction models. The US elects Donald Trump president in November; he takes office in January 2025. The global electoral calendar includes many other national contests whose outcomes matter at lower geographic levels but need not all appear here.

Shipping disruptions continue around the Red Sea; drought and restrictions affect the Panama Canal. The WMO later reports 2024 as the hottest calendar year in its series, roughly 1.55°C above the 1850–1900 baseline. One year's warming figure must not be misrepresented as a long-term threshold assessment. Climate change is an ongoing risk multiplier, not a single event that determines all later geography.

## 2025 — Rising investment, rearmament and contested trade relationships

AI investment expands dramatically; demand for chips, data centers, cooling and electricity grows. Cloud-based automation, robotics and agentic software evolve at different speeds. Economic effects cannot be reduced to a global unemployment figure. Governments and firms make competing investments in industrial capacity, energy, defense and digital technology.

Military expenditure rises according to SIPRI's subsequent 2026 assessment of 2025 spending. Geopolitical disputes, sanctions, border policy, international migration, shipping and climate hazards remain important. Trump's second US administration begins in January; policy effects vary by sector and country.

## 2026 — Through the November 17 cutoff only

The United States and Israel launch strikes against Iran on 28 February; Iran retaliates and the Strait of Hormuz becomes a central shipping and energy concern. Conflict and regional security remain unsettled. Global trade policy continues changing, with WTO forecasts carrying uncertainty rather than final results. A 2026 election returns projected Democratic majorities in both chambers of the US Congress on November 3, with individual races subject to counting and certification. Other states and countries enter the forthcoming alternate-history divergence with distinct institutions and interests.

AI investment and adoption are expanding, but widespread actual economy-wide replacement of human labor has **not** been established as a completed fact by this cutoff. The fictional December AGI announcement creates the subsequent rapid displacement scenario; it must not be projected backward into actual history.

## Cross-cutting foundations

**Political:** governance, emergency powers, elections, alliances, legitimacy and public trust differ across jurisdictions. **Economic:** debt, inflation, wages, trade, resource ownership, manufacturing and ports link places but create dependency. **Military:** conflicts shift stockpiles, budgets, skilled labor, migration and infrastructure. **Environmental:** heat, drought, coastal flooding and fires change risk unevenly. **Technological:** advanced capability depends on semiconductors, electricity, communications, maintenance, data and trained personnel. **Demographic:** aging, migration, housing and unequal access to services matter more to local resilience than national headlines alone.

### Evidence versus extrapolation

Dates and events above are pre-divergence history; connections to the 2030 fictional Plague or later Cascade are research hypotheses. Avoid treating a national outcome as a local one, or attributing speculative calamities to real people. The next layer asks what differs specifically across the Western Hemisphere.
