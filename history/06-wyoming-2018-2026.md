# Wyoming — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [Western United States](03-western-united-states-2018-2026.md). **Next:** [Wyoming ranchlands and unresolved reservation-area study](09-wyoming-local-2018-2026.md). Do not assume that the intended 'Diamond Ranch' has been identified.

## 2018–2019: Energy and ranchland structure

Wyoming's population is comparatively sparse, but its economy contains disproportionately large energy production relative to population. Coal mines, oil and natural gas fields, pipelines, electrical generation, rail freight, equipment yards and specialized workers coexist with cattle ranches, irrigated hayfields, tourism and state/federal employment. The legal status of federal public land, private land, state land and tribal lands is complex, and **one ranch owner does not possess the authority of a tribal nation**.

Wind exposure, long winter travel, snow, remote medical services and forage availability can matter to households regardless of national economic growth. County seats, school districts, sheriff offices, emergency medical providers, water rights and agricultural extensions form essential local networks.

## 2020–2021: Pandemic and rural health

COVID-19 reaches Wyoming despite its lower settlement density. Regional hospitals, long emergency driving distances, occupational exposure, livestock and industrial workplaces, and tourism create different dynamics than densely populated coastal metros. Federal and state public-health choices, vaccination, schools and business operations change over time.

Energy demand and prices fall sharply early in the pandemic; recovery and later commodity-price shocks alter state revenue and employment prospects. Ranching faces different constraints: feed, beef prices, drought, labor and transport. National policies and global commodity prices can change local household conditions without physically damaging a ranch.

## 2022–2023: Energy markets, drought and labor

War-related fuel and energy market disruption changes the context for Wyoming coal, oil and gas. Mine employment, production, profitability, state revenues and long-term demand are not interchangeable statistics. Water availability and grazing affect cattle herd size and hay production independently of natural-gas prices.

Wind energy and transmission projects grow in importance, but generating energy and exporting it through the grid are different capacities. Decades-old coal and gas facilities may have physically surviving equipment after a fictional grid disaster yet still lack trained operators, replacement parts or safe distribution.

## 2024–2026: Complex dependence despite low density

Wyoming remains a major US energy producer. The EIA's state overview reports that in **2025** it supplied about two-fifths of US coal output and was a significant net energy exporter; wind accounted for a substantial share of the state's electrical generation. EIA figures are year-specific and should not be projected unchanged to 2030 or 2085.

Commodity markets, public-land policy, ranch economics, property rights, wildfire, winter storms and tourism remain relevant to state and county decisions. Wyoming's rural economy is not universally self-sufficient: diesel, veterinary medicine, fertilizer, parts, food processing, machinery and hospital equipment arrive through national or international networks.

Federal, county, municipal and tribal jurisdictions continue to operate in different areas. Wyoming contains the Wind River Indian Reservation, home to the Eastern Shoshone and Northern Arapaho tribes, **but the separate worldbuilding reference to 'the reservation near Diamond Ranch' has not been identified**. Do not infer that it refers to Wind River without evidence from the world creator.

## Pre-divergence practical inventory

- **Ranching:** herd numbers, breeding, forage, seasonal hay, feed storage, fencing, veterinary care, water rights, irrigation and winter transport.
- **Energy:** mines, wells, pumps, compressor stations, pipelines, substations, transmission, fuel depots, technicians and rail connections.
- **Healthcare and geography:** hospital and ambulance travel time, small clinics, winter closure roads, rivers and mountain passes.
- **Institutions:** county commissioners, sheriff, schools, tribal governments, federal agencies, land-title and grazing-right relationships.
- **Trade:** where beef is slaughtered and processed, where machine parts arrive, and whether local workshops can replace imported tools.

## Why this matters to speculative post-Cascade Wyoming

Lower population density might reduce some introductions of a new epidemic, but medical distance and essential-work contact remain vulnerabilities. Ranching can survive only if water, feed, people and transport are sufficient; herds are not an unlimited food surplus. Energy abundance does not guarantee grid independence. Legal sovereignty does not transfer automatically to whichever estate is wealthiest.
