# Clatsop County and the northern Oregon coast — 2018 to 17 November 2026

**Parent:** [Oregon](05-oregon-2018-2026.md). **Focus:** Astoria, Warrenton, Gearhart, Seaside, Cannon Beach, lower Columbia estuary, coastal valleys and routes into the Coast Range. **Separate speculative study:** [Clatsop County after the Plague](../regions/clatsop-county.md).

## 2018–2019: A coast with trade and seasonal populations

The regional economy includes fishing, seafood processing, tourism, hospitality, timber, construction, retail, education, public services and specialized maritime work. Astoria and Warrenton are linked to the Columbia River estuary, port services and highway corridors. Seaside and Cannon Beach draw visitors from the Portland region and farther away; even small permanent populations can interact with large numbers of travelers seasonally.

The coast's geography matters: lower river mouths, tidal waterways, steep forested hills and low-lying developed districts produce different flood, landslide, evacuation and bridge risks. US 101 runs along the coast while US 26 and other roads provide routes inland. The Astoria–Megler Bridge connects Oregon to Washington across the Columbia. River transport is subject to estuary currents, weather, bar conditions, vessels and trained crews, not only the presence of open water.

## 2020: Tourism disruption and statewide wildfire pressure

COVID-19 disrupts visitor demand, food service, lodging, schools and medical operations. Tourism supports local wages but brings seasonal fluctuations in activity and population. Ports and coastal shipping create a different exposure pattern from ski resorts, ranches or dense city centers.

The September 2020 Oregon Labor Day wildfires have major statewide impacts. It is wrong to say that every coastal town burned in those fires: distinguish fire footprints, smoke transport, damage to inland roads, state emergency resources and direct local losses. A region may experience supply disruption without burning itself.

## 2021: Hazard planning and critical services

Clatsop County's **2021 Multi-Jurisdictional Natural Hazards Mitigation Plan** documents risks from Cascadia earthquakes and tsunamis, floods, landslides, erosion, wildfire, storms and other hazards. It identifies economic foundations in forestry, fishing, manufacturing and tourism. The plan is a snapshot from a particular year, not proof that each project was complete or each vulnerability solved by 2026.

Seaside provides an especially important example. The hazard plan lists multiple bridges potentially susceptible to damage in a major Cascadia Subduction Zone earthquake, including crossings over the Necanicum and Neawanna. It also notes that Seaside School District relocated three schools outside the tsunami zone. This real seismic planning is separate from the fictional **solar** Cascade, which does not necessarily generate a tsunami or earthquake.

County emergency response depends on roads, river crossings, radio, medical facilities, electricity, food distribution, tourism facilities, schools and neighboring jurisdictions.

## 2022–2024: Rising costs and institutional capacity

Inflation and housing pressures affect workers in visitor-facing and essential occupations. A coastal community may have a supply of vacation homes but not enough affordable permanent housing for nurses, teachers, tradespeople or seafood-industry workers. Marine activity depends on working docks, vessel repair, fuel, safety equipment, crews, buyers and preservation; timber work on equipment, road access, transport and forest management.

The 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome and later severe weather illustrate that Oregon's climate risks are broader than familiar rain and wind. Coastal marine influence typically differs from inland heat; historical weather records must be evaluated at actual stations rather than borrowed from Portland.

## 2025–November 2026: A working maritime community, not a ready-made successor kingdom

The historical baseline includes existing incorporated cities, county agencies, port districts, emergency responders, tribal nations with distinct rights, local civic organizations and regional trade. No successor monarchy, estate dynasty, port republic or independent territorial border exists as an established event of the documented 2018–2026 period.

Tourism and supply flows link the coast to the Portland and Willamette region. River trade links to southwest Washington and inland communities. These dependencies do not prove all connections fail simultaneously after the future Plague or Cascade.

## Local focus A: Astoria and the Columbia estuary

Astoria's waterfront, port and associated marine skills matter for ship maintenance, fisheries, river traffic, road/rail connections and food preservation. Strong tidal influences, estuary navigation, bridges, weather, docks and equipment condition constrain later navigation. Large ships are not automatically useful as coastal sailing vessels after fuel and crew shortages.

## Local focus B: Warrenton, Gearhart and lowland/coastal communities

Lowland flooding and estuary geography differ from steep upland timber terrain. Warrenton and nearby developed areas combine residential, tourism, port and service economies. Gearhart is a separate settlement with its own population and infrastructure, not an unimportant footnote to Seaside. Property development, drainage, road links, housing and medical access need local mapping.

## Local focus C: Seaside, Cannon Beach and inland routes

Seaside and Cannon Beach have different tourist infrastructure, coastal exposure and road networks. Coastal hospitality can provide useful buildings and supplies but is not equivalent to long-term farming. Seaside's river crossings, public works, water treatment and school relocation are critical baseline details.

Identify which routes survive bridge failure or extended power outages. A hazard plan may warn of physical earthquake destruction, but a fictional solar event should be modeled through power/communications and supply disruption, **not by automatically assigning tsunami damage**.

## Questions carried into alternate history

Who remains during off-season outbreaks? How many permanent residents versus visitors require food and care? Can boats operate with non-digital navigation and available fuel? How is catch preserved and salted? What portion of lowland acreage can be farmed without modern drainage or imported inputs? Which bridges and roads carry regional trade? What legal authorities and community organizations remain trusted?

Do not invent casualty numbers, port magnates, dynasties or rival polities before the resource and population baseline is established.
