# Successor societies and the rise of feudal-like America

**Status:** broad tendency established; individual regimes, borders and dynasties not yet invented.

## How decentralization can happen

Pandemic mortality undermines some metropolitan institutions and moves people toward surviving smaller settlements. The Cascade then disrupts the energy, transport and administrative networks of remaining large states. Territorial authority may concentrate around control of water, defensible sites, stored grain, arable land, mills, herds, markets and maintained roads. Some protection or tenancy agreements become inheritable over decades.

But **feudal-like** is an outcome requiring specific institutions, not a uniform aesthetic. Some societies keep elected councils and sheriffs; others have hereditary landed powers, city republics, military arrangements or federations. Tribal sovereignty and community institutions have separate continuity and should not be automatically subsumed under ranch elites.

## Institutions to trace

- Property and water rights; land abandonment and contested claims.
- Local law, militia, courts, taxation, contract and succession.
- Dependence versus voluntary cooperative institutions.
- Religion, education, archives, technical apprenticeship and historical memory.
- Trade, marriage alliances, migration, household formation and social mobility.
- Distinct political regimes across regions, including pockets of advanced technology.

## Fifty-year generational perspective

Year 50 elders may remember the pre-Plague world; middle generations grew up during collapse and reconstruction; younger adults may regard the successor system as normal. Existing flags, county names and constitutional language could persist even where officeholding practices change radically.

**Avoid assuming hereditary rulers appear overnight.** Establish what each leader actually controls, how it is supplied, how authority is recognized, how labor obligations arise and why heirs keep power.
