# The Great Plague

**Status:** established event and broad geographical pattern; pathogen, mortality rates and exact calendar chronology unresolved.

## Hypothesized global history

The Great Plague precedes the Cascade by several years and largely recedes before the first extraordinary solar sequence. International air travel initially distributes cases across widely separated population centers. Subsequent local transmission follows commuting and metropolitan contact networks. The major men's football World Cup of **2030**, hosted principally by Spain, Portugal and Morocco with centenary matches in South America, is a **potential** worldwide mixing/amplification event, not yet a proven in-world origin. The pathogen is not caused by the solar activity.

**Symptoms and transmission premise:** Severe disease becomes conspicuous, with a substantial share of transmission associated with recognizable illness, giving communities an opportunity to screen, isolate and restrict movement. This characteristic is not yet a defined disease model; too much presymptomatic spread would undermine the rural containment story, while zero early transmissibility complicates rapid international seeding. Develop consistent timing rather than inventing pathogen particulars casually.

## The demographic inversion

Highly connected city systems and international hubs are often hit early and repeatedly. Small towns, isolated valleys and ranch districts sometimes have extra warning and fewer corridors to close. They may avoid large epidemics **when** containment happens early and local conditions favor it. There are important exceptions: large cities can contain outbreaks; rural places can suffer disproportionate deaths if disease enters and healthcare is inadequate.

Do not equate “population reduction” with “plague deaths.” Track:
- Direct infection mortality;
- All-cause excess mortality before the Cascade;
- Migration, including pre-Cascade relocation while cars and air travel remain operational;
- Births and subsequent population changes;
- Later post-Cascade mortality, separately.

## Southern California

Los Angeles and the highly connected urbanized Inland Empire experience especially devastating early waves. Cross-jurisdictional road networks and commuting make a clean boundary impractical. The San Bernardino Mountains create more limited access routes toward Victor Valley and Big Bear, allowing earlier regional movement restrictions to have a better chance of working. **Cajon Pass is central but not the sole approach**: Big Bear/Lucerne and other roads require separate treatment. Neither high elevation nor desert climate confers immunity.

A comparatively less-devastated Victor Valley preserves population and skilled labor before the Cascade. Big Bear's tourist movement creates initial risk; the timing of visitor restrictions and arrivals must be modeled. Large pre- and post-event migrations complicate all eventual population counts. Whether the two regions' mortality differences are moderate or extreme is still open.

## Worldwide application

The same logic can recur in other countries, but do not copy identical outcomes into London, Tokyo, Lagos, Delhi, Mexico City, Paris or remote rural districts without examining their travel networks, health systems, settlement geography and timing.

## Open research tasks

1. Establish a credible qualitative disease course, infectiousness relative to symptom onset, outbreak duration and post-wave immunity, without claiming a real pathogen fits every requirement.
2. Identify plausibly effective isolation measures and their political, food-supply and economic costs.
3. Build regional demographic scenario ranges instead of unsupported fixed percentages.
4. Separate known contemporary mobility geography from invented alternate-history outcomes.
