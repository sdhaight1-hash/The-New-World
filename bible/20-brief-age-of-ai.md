# The Brief Age of AI (2022–2030)

The decades leading to the Great Plague are often approached through the disasters themselves, but the fundamental transformation of the late pre-collapse world begins earlier. Between the public arrival of conversational artificial intelligence in **November 2022** and the upheavals of **2030**, human societies undergo an unusually rapid change in the organization of labor, ownership, production, communication and political expectations.

For historical clarity, the period has two distinct evidentiary halves. **November 30, 2022 through November 17, 2026** follows documented real-world history, not an invented alternate history. **December 2026 onward** marks The New World's deliberately divergent chronology. The first decisive invented event is a convincing public announcement of artificial general intelligence in **December 2026**. Its announcing organization, exact day, system name, demonstrated abilities and international reception have not yet been established.

This era is *brief* because the Plague begins around 2030 and the Cascade follows approximately four or five years later. Artificial intelligence is not assumed to magically disappear. The global infrastructure, economic system and institutional organization that support its most intensive forms are instead progressively disrupted.

## 2022: The public opening

On **November 30, 2022**, OpenAI introduced ChatGPT as a publicly accessible research preview using a model from the GPT-3.5 family. **GPT-3 itself was introduced in 2020**, so the watershed here is specifically the public experience of conversational AI, not the first appearance of the GPT-3 model.

The importance of ChatGPT lay less in a single benchmark than in the ease with which individuals, students, businesses and institutions could interact with powerful generative software through ordinary language. For this chronology, the launch is the first landmark of the Age of AI.

## 2023–2024: Capabilities enter daily life

On **March 14, 2023**, OpenAI introduced GPT-4, demonstrating more capable language-based problem solving and multimodal model development. On **May 13, 2024**, GPT-4o brought faster, more natural interaction across text, voice and vision. Competing products and models proliferated across the industry. Schools, creative occupations, software development, administrative work and customer services encountered new tools and new disputes over how to use them.

These developments are established modern history. Their ultimate effect on The New World's later institutions is a separate historical interpretation.

## 2025–November 17, 2026: From assistance toward agents

AI products increasingly move beyond generating answers or content toward acting within software workflows. OpenAI's ChatGPT agent announcement on **July 17, 2025**, is one documented example of this direction. By 2026, organizations are experimenting with automating larger portions of office processes and using AI to augment or replace particular tasks.

The real-world labor evidence at this date is **mixed and uneven**, not proof that most workers have already been displaced. Stanford's August 2026 analysis finds pronounced relative employment weakness for young workers in AI-exposed jobs but does not find widespread economy-wide displacement. Other 2026 research likewise distinguishes observed adoption from theoretical automation capability. This distinction matters: the coming fictional acceleration is a **divergence from observed history**, not a retrospective claim about the real economy.

## December 2026: The announcement of AGI

**Established alternate-history turning point.** In December 2026 an artificial general intelligence is **convincingly announced to the world**. The exact meaning of general intelligence remains debatable, but demonstrations and independent scrutiny persuade enough governments, researchers, investors and the wider public that a qualitatively important threshold has been crossed.

The announcement marks the definitive divergence from the documented world. It should not be represented as an actual event that had happened as of November 17, 2026.

An announcement is not identical to instantaneous universal deployment. Access to computing power, energy, data, software permissions, industrial hardware and organizational trust still limits application. Those constraints determine which kinds of work are displaced first.

The announcement's **exact day, organization, model, demonstrations, regulation and immediate market response remain open** for future history.

## 2027: The first great displacement

**Established directional premise; detailed outcomes open.** Organizations racing to adopt increasingly capable general-purpose AI begin restructuring rather than merely adding software assistants to existing jobs. Work that is already digital, repetitive, reviewable or readily integrated with company systems changes most quickly. In customer support, clerical operations, routine analysis, content generation and portions of programming, fewer human employees can be required to produce the same or more output.

A distinctive early sign is the weakening of entry-level career ladders. When firms can automate junior tasks, fewer new workers learn the jobs through which older professionals acquired their expertise. The immediate danger is not only unemployment but the **loss of pathways into employment**.

Replacement is uneven. Occupations involving physical dexterity, direct care, legally accountable judgment, local trust or difficult environments remain constrained in different ways. Some workers become exceptionally productive with AI; others lose their position. This pattern is not the same as a uniform disappearance of labor.

## 2028: The automation boom

**Established directional premise; specifics remain to be researched.** The initial labor shock deepens into a worldwide reorganization of enterprises and public services. Robotics, automated warehouses, vehicle systems, industrial inspection, infrastructure monitoring and machine-managed logistics expand where capital and infrastructure permit. In many industries, profits and output can grow without proportional gains in wages or conventional employment.

Adoption is not geographically uniform. Wealthier corporations, capital-intensive regions and countries with strong grids, chips, factories and networks take advantage first; poorer and more fragile regions experience different mixtures of exclusion, import dependence, partial adoption and sudden job competition. Human labor is increasingly valued for either scarce technical skills or work that automation cannot yet perform economically.

The central social contradiction is a **high-capacity economy with a weakening wage-distribution system**. Communities depend on human paychecks for housing, healthcare, food, transport and taxes even as production requires fewer human workers. Productivity gains do not automatically become purchasing power for those displaced.

## 2029: Prosperity and fracture

**Established directional premise; specific laws, protests, crises and casualty counts not yet canon.** The automated economy reaches impressive but fragile heights in its most developed regions. Some households and institutions prosper, while others face unemployment, reduced hours, underemployment, loss of benefits and difficulty rebuilding a career. Public arguments intensify over who owns productive AI systems and how their benefits should be shared.

Governments encounter simultaneous pressures to support displaced citizens, regulate powerful systems, maintain legitimacy and protect tax revenues tied to employment and consumption. Possible responses include cash-transfer schemes, wage subsidies, public employment, retraining and changes in taxation, but **no universal global policy** is assumed, and individual countries' choices must be established separately.

Social consequences are locally specific. Families combine households; migration follows remaining employment; some small towns and regional centers gain residents, while other communities lose young workers. Housing patterns, access to services, business districts and political movements change before the Plague begins. None of these effects should be described through invented statistical certainty without demographic models.

## 2030: A world already weakened

The Great Plague begins in or around 2030 in the working chronology, with international travel a candidate amplifier. It does **not** arise because of AI, nor is AGI established as its biological cause.

The disease arrives after years of economic dislocation. Some people are unemployed or dependent on strained benefit systems. Others work in highly automated, geographically concentrated workplaces. Economic activity and travel remain global, but public trust and institutional capacity are uneven. Some medical systems have become more efficient through automation, while some communities have lost local staff, stable income and the means to access care.

These conditions alter both **exposure** and **resilience**. Automated logistics may preserve supply temporarily; dependence on centralized software and highly specialized infrastructure can also create bottlenecks. Regions with continuing human skills, local food, independent utilities and trusted institutions may gain resilience. This is not a guarantee that rural areas survive or that automated systems always fail.

The Plague then inflicts its own direct mortality and population movements. Years later, the separate Cascade and Long Storms damage the power and communications networks on which centralized AI production, cloud computing and industrial automation depend.

## Legacy after the Cascade

The brief Age of AI leaves contradictory inheritances:

- Some facilities, robots, software, documentation, datasets and machine tools survive physically, but reliable electricity, networks, parts, specialist maintenance and replacement components become scarce.
- Some systems may remain useful on isolated or repaired local infrastructure. **AI does not vanish from physics** when cloud networks fail; surviving hardware and locally runnable models could matter enormously.
- Communities that surrendered human expertise to automated processes may struggle to reconstruct work without those systems. Communities retaining craftspeople, technicians, farmers and institutional memory may have different advantages.
- Infrastructure built for highly optimized automated supply chains can be powerful when intact and brittle when its dependencies fail.
- Pre-Plague unemployment, inequality and legitimacy crises shape who owned equipment, who held land, which families migrated and what people expected from institutions before epidemic mortality and later infrastructure collapse.

**Core historical sequence:** mass-market conversational AI → accelerated capabilities and agents → December 2026 AGI announcement → rapid automation and broad labor displacement → social and fiscal fracture → Great Plague amid pre-existing instability → partial recovery → Cascade and long-term infrastructure fragility → divergent successor societies.

The *Age of AI* is therefore a precondition for the later world, **not a third unrelated physical catastrophe** and not a replacement for the independent causes of the Great Plague and the Cascade.
